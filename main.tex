\section{Summary of the \cite{andre2024}}
\label{sec:Summary of the Focal Paper}

\subsection{Motivation and Research Question}
\label{subsec:Motivation and Research Question}

    Given the threats of Global Warming, as consolidated in the \textcite{ipcc2022} report, it is essential to understand where Climate Change originates and design effective environmental policies and international agreements to tackle it. Therefore, policymakers deciding on those environmental policies should have access to all the necessary information about human behaviour, including beliefs, attitudes, motivations, and social interaction, to design them in such a way that they are accepted by the general public and institutions.

    In this context, it is essential to understand what determines an individual's willingness to fight Global Warming. \textcite{andre2024} addressed this issue by examining how individuals perceive social norms\footnote{Social norms are guidelines for behaviour in social situations. They limit the arbitrariness of such behaviour through mutual expectations and ensure that social interactions and relationships can be planned.} and how this perception affects their willingness to fight Global Warming. They focused on three dimensions of climate behaviour: \emph{(i)} the individual pro-climate behaviour (measured by an incentivised donation decision), \emph{(ii)} their beliefs about others’ behaviour and normative views and \emph{(iii)} their support for environmental policies. Based on the measured results, they were able to address the problem of misperceived social norms influencing climate behaviour. They then wanted to find out whether correcting these misperceptions could increase individuals' support for environmental policies.
    
\subsection{Methods and Identification Strategy}
\label{subsec:Methods and Identification Strategy}

    \textcite{andre2024} measure individuals' willingness to fight Global Warming by conducting a quota-based\footnote{Targeting the US adult population (2019)} survey of 8,000 participants via a donation decision of an incentivised task. They also collected detailed information about economic preferences, moral foundations and baseline characteristics of the participants. The measurements were used for a preliminary descriptive analysis of the determinants of climate behaviour and the misperceptions of social norms among 2,000 participants. A randomised controlled trial (RCT) was conducted as a follow-up information experiment on the 6,000 other participants, in which two information treatments were used to identify a causal relation between perceived social norms as key drivers for climate behaviour.

\subsection{Main Results and Conclusions}
\label{subsec:Main Results and Conclusions}

    Perceived social norms and economic preferences are statistically significant predictors of \mbox{climate behaviour}. It was found that Americans systematically underestimate the prevalence of climate-related social norms. The information experiment showed that informing respondents about the actual prevalence reduced these misperceptions and encouraged individuals to adopt \mbox{climate-friendly} behaviour. This effect is especially large among participants who are sceptical about global warming, a group that is generally difficult to reach.
    
    \begin{keymessage}[To Sum Up]
        Many people act not out of conviction, but because they mistakenly believe that others are doing nothing. Correcting misperceived social norms could trigger a positive feedback loop where learning about the existing support of climate norms encourages Americans to take visible action against Climate Change, which encourages others to follow suit.
    \end{keymessage}
    
    In an early Policy Letter, \textcite{andre2021} summarised their main findings, which are aimed at decision-makers in politics and government. A concurrent study done by \textcite{andre2024globally} was able to replicate these results in a worldwide context, making information treatments a unique solution for prompting and scaling pro-climate behaviour: a simple, scalable and cost-effective intervention.

\section{Positioning within Course Themes and Literature}
\label{sec:Positioning within Course Themes and Literature}

\subsection{Central Behavioural Mechanism: Perceived Social Norms}
\label{subsec:Central Behavioural Mechanism}

    The central behavioural mechanism studied by \textcite{andre2024} is a causal chain: individuals hold systematically biased \emph{second-order beliefs} about the climate-related behaviour and moral approval of their fellow citizens; correcting these beliefs shifts their \emph{perceived social norm}, which in turn raises their own climate action and policy support. The mechanism thus runs from perceived norms to behaviour, with misperception as the friction the intervention removes.
    
    This presupposes that climate decisions are not made in isolation. \textcite{GANGULI2026} show that individuals act as conditional cooperators, whose willingness to reduce emissions depends on their beliefs about others. \textcite{andre2024} identify the failure mode of this logic: Americans systematically \emph{underestimate} how widespread pro-climate norms actually are. This ``pluralistic ignorance'' anchors conditional cooperation to an artificially pessimistic reference point, dampening climate action even where latent support is high.
    
    Furthermore, it is important to distinguish social norms from conventional behavioural interventions. \cite{gravert2021} explicitly contrast simple nudges with norms and monetary incentives, showing that nudges alone are often insufficient for long-term behavioural shifts such as habit formation in public transport. Instead, habit formation relies heavily on what is perceived as the societal norm. 
    
    The paper thus does not challenge the view that norms matter—it \emph{refines} it. The binding constraint, it shows, is frequently not the \emph{absence} of pro-climate norms but their systematic \emph{misperception}.
    By addressing the fundamental misperception of these norms, \cite{andre2024} tap into a distinct and powerful mechanism that goes beyond traditional nudging, providing a structural foundation for long-term behavioural change and policy acceptance.

\subsection{Relation to Behavioural Climate Policy Literature}
\label{subsec:Relation to Behavioural Climate Policy Literature}

    The focal paper contributes to the political economy of climate policy, where a central puzzle is the weak public support for economically 
    efficient instruments such as carbon pricing. \textcite{Mildenberger2022} show that even redistributive design features of returning carbon tax 
    revenue as rebates did not boost overall public support\footnote{Mainfinding 2 of Sophia Kaindl, 15 June 2026}. So if financial self-interest does not reliably move acceptance, the question becomes what does. \textcite{andre2024} offers the behavioural answer of correcting misperceived social norms.

    Scholars, like \textcite{gravert2022}, state that 'Nudges compensate failures of carbon taxes and improve their effectiveness'\footnote{Result of Jonas Lehmköster, 03.13.2026}. \textcite{andre2024} extend this literature by identifying a specific, highly potent nudge: the correction of pluralistic ignorance.
    
    Finally, the paper extends work on mass support for climate policy.\textcite{bechtel2013} explain support for global climate agreements primarily through institutional design (cost distribution, enforcement, participation). \textcite{andre2024} add a behavioural layer: mass support depends not only on the objective features of a 
    policy but on what citizens \emph{believe} other citizens want. By demonstrating that these beliefs are systematically biased and causally correctable, the paper identifies a behavioural market failure that prior institutional accounts had overlooked.
    
    In sum, the contribution of \textcite{andre2024} is primarily \emph{empirical and methodological}. The gap it fills is the missing causal link between the well-documented relevance of social norms and the concrete design of scalable policy interventions, especially for climate sceptics, a group typically considered unreachable.

    Yet norm-correction addresses only the \emph{domestic} dimension of the 
    problem. Even a population fully aware of prevailing pro-climate norms 
    still faces the underlying structure of climate change as a global 
    collective-action problem, in which national self-interest invites 
    free-riding \parencite{bechtel2013}. Whether shifting perceived norms 
    can translate into support for the binding, internationally coordinated 
    instruments that this structure demands are ultimately a question of 
    Policy design is the focus of the following chapter.

    Importantly, the mechanism is distinct from conventional nudging. 
    \textcite{gravert2021} find that simple nudges are often insufficient for 
    durable behavioural change, which instead relies on what people perceive 
    as normal. \textcite{andre2024} therefore do not merely add another 
    nudge; by targeting the \emph{belief} that sustains the norm, they engage 
    a more structural determinant of behaviour.

\section{Policy Relevance and Regulatory Context}
\label{sec:Policy Relevance and Regulatory Context}

\subsection{Policy Domain and Current Landscape}
\label{subsec:Policy Domain and Current Landscape}

%Policy Domain
    It is only in climate diplomacy that reciprocity is largely set aside in favour of voluntary commitments. 
    Behavioural economics highlights additional drivers of this gap: present bias, limited attention, status quo bias, and the power of social norms. Social norms and reciprocity strongly shape climate-relevant behaviour; people are more willing to act when they believe others also contribute. Misperceptions of prevailing norms (pluralistic ignorance) can constitute a behavioural market failure that dampens climate action. Ethical governance requires transparency and accuracy; the goal is correcting false beliefs, not secretly manipulating preferences.
%Current landscape
    The current climate policy landscape is defined by the fundamental challenge of global cooperation. Currently, most international climate goals are being missed because voluntary commitments fail to overcome the inherent free-rider problem of global warming. As \textcite{ockenfels2023_ECONtributePB52} highlights, climate protection must be viewed as a basic public good—akin to public infrastructure, streets, or security. To solve this, Ockenfels argues that international negotiations must shift away from purely voluntary pledges towards reciprocal, mutual obligations. This entails establishing a "climate club" of willing nations that agree on a minimal, reciprocal carbon price, stabilised through cross-border payments and sanctions, while simultaneously ensuring that green technologies become cheaper than fossil fuels. Regional efforts are necessary but ultimately insufficient without this international reciprocity.

    While \textcite{ockenfels2023_ECONtributePB52} addresses the macro-institutional design of climate policy, these structural solutions heavily depend on the behavioural mechanisms explored by \textcite{andre2024}. The free-rider problem exists not only between nations but also among individuals. Just as states require reciprocal commitments to act decisively, individuals base their climate-friendly behaviour on the perceived actions and norms of their peers. \textcite{andre2024} demonstrate that widespread "pluralistic ignorance"—where individuals systematically underestimate the climate-friendly norms of their fellow citizens—acts as a "behavioural market failure." This misperception artificially dampens public support for precisely the kind of robust pricing policies and international climate clubs that \textcite{ockenfels2023_ECONtributePB52} advocates. 

    Within Europe, this interplay between macro-regulation and micro-behaviour is highly relevant for the evolving regulatory landscape. The EU is currently preparing a new integrated framework for European climate resilience and risk management \parencite{EC_ResilienceFramework_Initiative_2026}, building upon the European Green Deal and the EU Adaptation Strategy (past evaluations, public consultation, and was first announced in the European Green Deal in 2019). This forthcoming framework aims to introduce concepts like "climate resilience by design" and clear risk ownership across sectors. 

    To successfully implement such comprehensive policies—which inherently require a mix of economic instruments (like the Emissions Trading System), strict regulations, and subsidies-policymakers must secure broad public backing. Traditional climate policy tools remain central, but they can be significantly enhanced by behavioural instruments. As derived from the focal paper, correcting misperceived social norms serves as a low-cost, scalable tool to strengthen the "license to regulate." By systematically integrating norm-based information into national and communal campaigns, policymakers can align public perception with actual majorities, thereby increasing the political feasibility of both local adaptation measures and international pricing regimes.

    Specifically, the focal paper directly speaks to the policy domain of social norm campaigns and public information disclosure policies. In practice, interventions relying on social comparisons have already proven effective in the energy sector. For instance, the 'Opower' programmes in the US and parts of Europe successfully reduced household energy consumption by including social comparisons in utility bills, showing consumers how their energy use compares to their neighbours. However, on a broader national or EU regulatory level, communication strategies still predominantly focus on the urgency of the crisis (e.g., the European Climate Pact's emphasis on scientific facts) rather than systematically correcting normative misperceptions. At the same time, the regulatory landscape is expanding its economic instruments, such as the introduction of the EU ETS 2, which will price emissions from buildings and road transport starting in 2027. Introducing such direct carbon pricing mechanisms significantly affects citizens' daily lives and faces high risks of political backlash.

\subsection{Refine existing Policy and Practical Feasibility}
\label{subsec:Refine existing Policy and Practical Feasibility}

    The European Commission is currently developing the **European Climate Resilience and Risk Management – Integrated Framework**, a new policy initiative designed to help EU Member States better prepare for and prevent the escalating impacts of climate change. Slated for adoption in the second half of 2026, the framework aims to establish a more ambitious, comprehensive, and coherent approach to climate resilience across the EU. It responds to calls from the European Parliament and Council for bolder climate action and builds upon the existing 2021 EU Adaptation Strategy. The framework is being shaped by a recently closed public consultation, which highlighted strong stakeholder support for proactive measures such as "resilience-by-design" in investments, harmonized climate risk assessments, and nature-based solutions. Ultimately, the initiative seeks to safeguard Europe's security, prosperity, and public health against future climate-related extremes.
    

    The European \emph{climate resilience and risk management -- integrated framework} is a forthcoming EU initiative pushed by \textcite{EC_ResilienceFramework_Initiative_2026} that aims to establish a more ambitious, comprehensive and coherent approach to climate resilience and preparedness across all Member States and at EU level.

    There is also a Call for Action paper by \cite{EC_ResilienceFramework_CallForEvidence_2025} and an Factual Summary by the \cite{EC_ResilienceFramework_FactualSummary_2026}.

    Its core objective is to help prevent and prepare for the growing impacts of Climate Change by creating a balanced policy package that combines legislative and non-legislative measures, economic instruments, and information tools, with adoption planned for the second half of 2026. The framework builds on existing EU adaptation strategies and responds to calls from the European Parliament, the Council and the European Court of Auditors for stronger action on climate risks.

    Key elements already announced include a common climate reference scenario for Europe based on a 3\(^\circ\)C global warming trajectory, which will serve as a benchmark for EU policies and provide a harmonised methodology for climate risk assessment plans by Member States, sectors and private actors. The initiative is also expected to introduce the concepts of ``climate resilience by design'' and ``risk ownership'', meaning that resilience considerations will be systematically integrated into policies, investments and planning, and that responsibilities for action will be clearly allocated in key sectors such as public health, energy, transport and defence.

    In addition, the framework will provide decision-support tools based on Copernicus data to help stakeholders identify climate impacts in their regions or sectors, and will pay particular attention to closing finance and insurance protection gaps, given that only about 25\% of climate-related losses in Europe are currently insured.
