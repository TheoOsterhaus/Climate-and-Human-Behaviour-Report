\subsection{Methods and Identification Strategy}
\label{subsec:Methods and Identification Strategy}

    They measure individuals' willingness to fight Global Warming by conducting a quota-based survey of 8.000 participants representing the adult US population. \textcite{andre2024} measure it via a donation decision of an incentivised task. IN addition to that, they collect detailed information about economic preferences, moral foundations and baseline characteristics of the participants. The survey comprised a descriptive analysis of the determinants of climate change behaviour and the misperceptions of social norms among 2,000 participants. A follow-up information experiment (RCT) was also conducted, in which two information treatments were used to identify statistically significant effects on donations, subsequent behaviour and norm beliefs, as well as treatment effect heterogeneity.

\subsection{Main Results and Conclusions}
\label{subsec:Main Results and Conclusions}
    \textcite{andre2024} identified a causal relation between perceived social norms as key drivers for climate change behaviour. They found that the vast majority of Americans underestimate the prevalence of climate-related social norms. Informing respondents about the actual prevalence can reduce these misperceptions and encourage individuals to adopt climate-friendly behaviour. This effect is especially large for participants who are sceptical about the existence of Global Warming, the group of people who are commonly difficult to reach.
    Many people act not out of conviction, but because they mistakenly believe that others are doing nothing. In sum, these measures could trigger a positive feedback loop where learning about the existing support of climate norms encourages Americans to take visible action against climate change, which encourages others to follow suit.

\subsection{Central Behavioural Mechanisms}
\label{subsec:Central Behavioural Mechanisms}

To fully contextualise the findings of \textcite{andre2024}, it is crucial to understand the underlying behavioural mechanisms that drive pro-environmental decision-making. Climate decisions are rarely made in a vacuum; rather, individuals are heavily embedded in social contexts. As \cite{GANGULI2026} demonstrate, social influence and conditional cooperation are foundational drivers of carbon dioxide mitigation efforts. People calibrate their own climate-friendly actions based on the observed or assumed behaviour of their peers.

This conditional nature of climate action is deeply intertwined with concerns about personal and social identity. \cite{SHERIF2025} highlight the trade-offs between impact, inspiration, and image in pro-environmental behaviours. They suggest that individuals are motivated to maintain a positive social image. Consequently, when the perceived normative baseline shifts---i.e., when individuals realise that a broad majority actually cares about climate change---acting sustainably becomes a prerequisite for maintaining a positive social standing. Correcting pluralistic ignorance, as done in the focal paper, essentially sets a new, pro-climate baseline for what is considered socially desirable.

Furthermore, it is important to distinguish social norms from conventional behavioural interventions. \cite{gravert2021} explicitly contrast simple nudges with norms and monetary incentives, showing that nudges alone are often insufficient for long-term behavioural shifts such as habit formation in public transport. Instead, habit formation relies heavily on what is perceived as the societal norm. By addressing the fundamental misperception of these norms, \cite{andre2024} tap into a distinct and powerful mechanism that goes beyond traditional nudging, providing a structural foundation for long-term behavioural change and policy acceptance.

\section{Policy Relevance and Regulatory Context}
\label{sec:Policy Relevance and Regulatory Context}

\subsection{Policy Domain and Current Landscape}
\label{subsec:Policy Domain and Current Landscape}

The current climate policy landscape is defined by the fundamental challenge of global cooperation. Currently, most international climate goals are being missed because voluntary commitments fail to overcome the inherent free-rider problem of global warming. As \textcite{ockenfels2023_ECONtributePB52} highlights, climate protection must be viewed as a basic public good—akin to public infrastructure, streets, or security. To solve this, Ockenfels argues that international negotiations must shift away from purely voluntary pledges towards reciprocal, mutual obligations. This entails establishing a "climate club" of willing nations that agree on a minimal, reciprocal carbon price, stabilised through cross-border payments and sanctions, while simultaneously ensuring that green technologies become cheaper than fossil fuels. Regional efforts are necessary but ultimately insufficient without this international reciprocity.

While \textcite{ockenfels2023_ECONtributePB52} addresses the macro-institutional design of climate policy, these structural solutions heavily depend on the behavioural mechanisms explored by \textcite{andre2024}. The free-rider problem exists not only between nations but also among individuals. Just as states require reciprocal commitments to act decisively, individuals base their climate-friendly behaviour on the perceived actions and norms of their peers. \textcite{andre2024} demonstrate that widespread "pluralistic ignorance"—where individuals systematically underestimate the climate-friendly norms of their fellow citizens—acts as a "behavioural market failure." This misperception artificially dampens public support for precisely the kind of robust pricing policies and international climate clubs that \textcite{ockenfels2023_ECONtributePB52} advocates. 

Within Europe, this interplay between macro-regulation and micro-behaviour is highly relevant for the evolving regulatory landscape. The EU is currently preparing a new integrated framework for European climate resilience and risk management \parencite{EC_ResilienceFramework_Initiative_2026}, building upon the European Green Deal and the EU Adaptation Strategy (past evaluations, public consultation, and was first announced in the European Green Deal in 2019). This forthcoming framework aims to introduce concepts like "climate resilience by design" and clear risk ownership across sectors. 

To successfully implement such comprehensive policies—which inherently require a mix of economic instruments (like the Emissions Trading System), strict regulations, and subsidies-policymakers must secure broad public backing. Traditional climate policy tools remain central, but they can be significantly enhanced by behavioural instruments. As derived from the focal paper, correcting misperceived social norms serves as a low-cost, scalable tool to strengthen the "license to regulate." By systematically integrating norm-based information into national and communal campaigns, policymakers can align public perception with actual majorities, thereby increasing the political feasibility of both local adaptation measures and international pricing regimes.

Specifically, the focal paper directly speaks to the policy domain of social norm campaigns and public information disclosure policies. In practice, interventions relying on social comparisons have already proven effective in the energy sector. For instance, the 'Opower' programmes in the US and parts of Europe successfully reduced household energy consumption by including social comparisons in utility bills, showing consumers how their energy use compares to their neighbours. However, on a broader national or EU regulatory level, communication strategies still predominantly focus on the urgency of the crisis (e.g., the European Climate Pact's emphasis on scientific facts) rather than systematically correcting normative misperceptions. At the same time, the regulatory landscape is expanding its economic instruments, such as the introduction of the EU ETS 2, which will price emissions from buildings and road transport starting in 2027. Introducing such direct carbon pricing mechanisms significantly affects citizens' daily lives and faces high risks of political backlash.


Behavioural economics highlights additional drivers of this gap: present bias, limited attention, status quo bias, and the power of social norms.
Social norms and reciprocity strongly shape climate-relevant behaviour; people are more willing to act when they believe others also contribute.
 Misperceptions of prevailing norms (pluralistic ignorance) can constitute a behavioural market failure that dampens climate action.
Ethical governance requires transparency and accuracy; the goal is correcting false beliefs, not secretly manipulating preferences.



\subsection{Implementation Status and Practical Feasibility}
\label{subsec:Implementation Status and Practical Feasibility}




The European \emph{climate resilience and risk management -- integrated framework} is a
forthcoming EU initiative pushed by \textcite{EC_ResilienceFramework_Initiative_2026} that aims to establish a more ambitious, comprehensive and coherent approach to climate resilience and preparedness across all Member States and at EU level.

There is also a Call for Action paper by \cite{EC_ResilienceFramework_CallForEvidence_2025} and an Factual Summary by the \cite{EC_ResilienceFramework_FactualSummary_2026}.

Its core objective is to help prevent and prepare for the growing impacts of climate change by creating a balanced policy package that combines legislative and non-legislative measures, economic instruments, and information tools, with adoption planned for the second half of 2026. The framework builds on existing EU adaptation strategies and responds to calls from the European Parliament, the Council and the European Court of Auditors for stronger action on climate risks.

Key elements already announced include a common climate reference scenario for Europe
based on a 3\(^\circ\)C global warming trajectory, which will serve as a benchmark for
EU policies and provide a harmonised methodology for climate risk assessment plans by
Member States, sectors and private actors. The initiative is also expected to introduce
the concepts of ``climate resilience by design'' and ``risk ownership'', meaning that
resilience considerations will be systematically integrated into policies, investments
and planning, and that responsibilities for action will be clearly allocated in key
sectors such as public health, energy, transport and defence.

In addition, the framework will provide decision-support tools based on Copernicus data
to help stakeholders identify climate impacts in their regions or sectors, and will pay
particular attention to closing finance and insurance protection gaps, given that only
about 25\% of climate-related losses in Europe are currently insured.
